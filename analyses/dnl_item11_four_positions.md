# Item 11 — four positions on Denali's capital intensity

*Denali Industries (DNL), Muddle Through, valuation date 25 May 2026. Every figure is
generated by `scripts/size_horizon_variants.py` from the assumption sets declared in
`design/methodology/horizon_variant_sets.yaml`, and is the same arithmetic that fuels
`analyses/dnl_item11_capital_intensity.xlsx`. WACC 8.877%, terminal growth 2.50%.*

---

## What is actually at issue

The explicit period runs ten years. Over those ten years Denali's revenue compounds 63.7%.
The question nobody has answered is how much capital the business has to put back in to
achieve that, and the four candidate answers are worth between $1.62 and $2.70 a share —
a spread of about a third of the company.

The question is not academic, and it is not a rounding-level tidy-up. It arises because
two numbers in the file are mutually inconsistent for a business growing at 6.155%
nominal, and the build has so far resolved the inconsistency by silently picking one.
Denali's invested capital at the valuation date is 108.3% of revenue, of which 94.5
points is fixed capital — net property, plant and equipment plus intangibles. Its
depreciation runs at 7.3% of revenue. Simple arithmetic: to grow a fixed base of $3,213m
at 6.155% while replacing what depreciates, year one needs capex of **$452.9m, or 12.55%
of revenue**. The file's capex anchor is 7%, which in year one is $283.7m.

Those two cannot both be right. Either the capex anchor understates what Denali will
actually spend, or the invested-capital base overstates what Denali actually needs to
run the business at this scale. Item 11 is a choice between them, and the four positions
below are four ways of making it.

A note on what follows. Each position is described first as arithmetic, then measured,
then argued about. The arithmetic sections are deliberately explicit — the whole reason
the last two sittings went wrong is that the arithmetic lived in a scratch file nobody
could see.

## The DuPont framework, briefly

DuPont analysis is the practice of not accepting a return ratio as a single fact. Any
return is a product of factors, and decomposing it tells you *which* factor is doing the
work — which in turn tells you what you would have to believe for the return to persist.

The original 1920s formulation was for return on equity, split three ways:

1. **Net margin** — net income ÷ revenue. How much of each dollar of sales survives to
   the bottom line.
2. **Asset turnover** — revenue ÷ assets. How hard the balance sheet is worked.
3. **Equity multiplier** — assets ÷ equity. How much of the balance sheet is borrowed.

Multiply the three and the revenue and asset terms cancel, leaving net income ÷ equity.
The point is not the algebra; it is that two firms reporting an identical 15% return on
equity can be entirely different businesses. One earns it on fat margins and a lazy
balance sheet, another on thin margins and rapid turns, a third on leverage alone. The
identity makes you say which.

For an enterprise valuation the leverage term is not wanted — the whole point of valuing
the firm rather than the equity is to strip financing out — so the useful form is the
two-factor version on invested capital:

> **ROIC = NOPAT margin × capital turnover**
>
> where NOPAT margin = after-tax operating profit ÷ revenue, and capital turnover =
> revenue ÷ invested capital.

Read it as: profitability per dollar of sales, multiplied by sales per dollar of capital
tied up. A business improves its return either by widening the first or by working the
second harder, and the two have very different implications. Margin improvement is a
claim about pricing power, cost position or mix. Turnover improvement is a claim about
physical productivity — the same plant producing more, or the same revenue needing less
plant.

That distinction is what makes DuPont the right tool here. All four positions below share
identical revenue, margins and tax. They differ only in the denominator of the turnover
term. So every difference in return between them is a turnover difference by construction,
and the honest question about each is whether the physical productivity it implies is one
you would put your name to.

One caution. Both factors depend on how invested capital is defined, and the definition
here is D-44's: net PP&E plus intangibles plus non-cash working capital, goodwill
excluded. Change that definition and every ROIC below moves. Denali's opening return is
10.09% on the D-44 base, 13.11% on PP&E plus working capital, and 6.81% once goodwill is
included. None of these is wrong; they answer different questions. The D-44 base asks
what return the operating business earns on the capital an operator would have to
replicate, which is the one relevant to a terminal.

## The 6–7% capex anchor — what it is, and what it will bear

Since three of the four positions are arguments about whether to believe this number, it
is worth being precise about its provenance.

The anchor lives in `data/companies/dnl.yaml` as `capex_pct_revenue: 0.07`, with this
rationale:

> Pre-demerger historical pattern (6-7%). FY25 reported 12.8% is inflated by partial-year
> reporting and capital restructuring; rejected as the steady-state anchor.

And in `data/financials/dnl.yaml`, against the FY25 actuals of $474.2m capex on $3,710.1m
revenue:

> Capex / revenue ratio inflated by FY25 partial-year reporting and capital-restructuring
> items. Underlying normalised capex / revenue is closer to 6-7% per pre-demerger
> historical pattern.

Four observations, in ascending order of importance.

**It carries no evidence reference and no computed series.** `evidence_refs` is empty. The
figure is a stated pattern, not a derivation — there is no year-by-year capex-to-revenue
table anywhere in the repository from which 6–7% falls out. The pre-demerger annual
reports are in `data/financials/historical/dnl/` as PDFs and have not been extracted. This
is not an accusation of error; normalising away an obviously distorted FY25 was the right
instinct. But the number that replaced it is currently an assertion, and it is now
carrying more weight than any other single input in the Denali build.

**The pattern is Incitec Pivot's, not Denali's.** "Pre-demerger" means the combined
entity, fertilisers and explosives together. Denali is the post-demerger explosives
business. A capital intensity observed across a group that included ammonia and fertiliser
manufacturing is not self-evidently the capital intensity of the residual, and nothing in
the file argues that it is.

**Depreciation was anchored to it, not observed against it.** The `da_pct_revenue`
rationale reads: "Matches assumed capex/revenue ratio of 7%, consistent with a steady-state
mature industrial where the asset base isn't growing fast." So the 7.3% depreciation rate
was set to agree with the 7% capex assumption. The file does add a genuine cross-check —
FY25 depreciation of $284.1m on $3,710.1m of revenue is 7.66%, "in line" — which is
reassuring, but it does not undo the direction of the inference. And this matters because
D-38 then converges the capex path onto D&A, and D-39 strikes the terminal from the final
explicit year. The chain runs: an assumed capex rate sets the depreciation rate, the
depreciation rate becomes the convergence target, and the convergence target produces a
terminal in which the asset base is flat. One unsourced number is doing all of it.

**Read carefully, it is a normalisation statement, not a steady-state claim.** The
surrounding prose is about the FY25 distortion unwinding — net debt easing "over the next
12 months as post-demerger integration completes and the capex / revenue ratio normalises".
That is a claim about the next year or two. It is not a claim about the capital intensity
required to run a business whose revenue is 64% larger, and it should not be read as one.
There is a difference between "this company's underlying capex is 7% of today's revenue"
and "this company can grow 64% while spending 7% of revenue", and the build has quietly
converted the first into the second.

The honest summary is that the anchor is probably about right for the next two or three
years and says nothing at all about year ten. That is not a reason to discard it. It is a
reason to stop extrapolating it.

## Position 1 — the ruled build

### The arithmetic

Capex is a declared path, not a derived one. It starts at 7.86% of revenue in year one
and glides linearly to 7.30% by year five, holding there through year ten (D-38). Because
depreciation is also 7.30% of revenue, capex less depreciation is exactly zero from year
five onward: every dollar spent replaces a dollar consumed, and nothing is added.

The terminal is then struck so the fixed capital base compounds at *g* forever, which
requires terminal capex of depreciation plus *g* times the base, landing at 8.77% of
revenue. Note the discontinuity that creates — the explicit period spends 7.30% and the
terminal 8.77%, and the step between them is the reinvestment the explicit period never
made.

### What it produces

| | |
|---|---|
| Value per share | **$2.6956** |
| Capex, year 10 | 7.30% of revenue · 33.3% of EBITDA |
| Terminal capex | 8.77% of revenue · 40.1% of EBITDA |
| Invested capital ÷ revenue, opening → year 10 | 108.3% → **72.6%** |
| Fixed capital, nominal | $3,213m → $3,275m (**+1.9%** over ten years) |
| Fixed capital, in real terms | **−20.4%** |
| Terminal ROIC | **14.94%**, or **1.68× WACC** |
| Terminal reinvestment rate | 17.2% of NOPAT |
| Return on *new* capital in the terminal | 14.58% |
| Terminal share of enterprise value | 51.3% |

### DuPont

| | NOPAT margin | × | Capital turnover | = | ROIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| At the valuation date | 10.93% | | 0.9236× | | 10.09% |
| In the terminal | 10.59% | | **1.4115×** | | 14.94% |

The margin *falls*. Operating margin does improve — 14.10% to 14.60% of revenue, the
transformation programme net of the gas roll-off — but the tax glide more than consumes
it. Hold turnover at its opening level and let only the margin move, and ROIC would be
9.78%, *below* where it starts. Hold the margin and let only turnover move, and ROIC would
be 15.42%.

So more than all of the improvement is capital turnover, and turnover rises 53%. That is
the entire case: the business is asserted to work its asset base half again as hard.

### Strengths

The capex rate is the one the company's own file supports, and at 7.30% converging from
7.86% it sits at the top of the stated 6–7% range rather than the bottom. Nobody has to
believe anything heroic about year-by-year spending. On the EBITDA measure the terminal
reinvests 40.1%, against the 50% five-year average capex-to-EBITDA the company file
records — so on the metric the file itself keeps, the terminal looks conservative. That
comparison should be read with one eye open: a five-year average ending in FY25 includes
the inflated year the rest of the file disowns, so 50% is a generous benchmark.

It is also internally coherent. The terminal reinvestment rate of 17.2% against growth of
2.50% implies a 14.58% return on new capital, essentially the same as the 14.94% on the
whole base. The terminal is not quietly assuming two different returns.

And Denali is genuinely pursuing an asset-light shift: the strategy block describes
"technology-led services growth", with bolt-on acquisitions in electronic initiation and
AI-driven blast design. Some rise in turnover is the strategy working.

### Weaknesses

Test the position against physical output rather than money. Industry volume growth (B25)
is 3.28%, which compounds to +38.0% of volume over ten years, delivered on a fixed asset
base that has shrunk 20.4% in real terms. That implies output per unit of real fixed
capital rising **73.3%** — about 5.7% a year, sustained for a decade, at a mature
industrial explosives manufacturer whose own file records the Louisiana ammonia plant
cost overruns of 2017–2020 and describes capital project execution as "the historical
weakness". Nothing in the repository supports that rate of asset productivity gain, and
the impact matrix does not claim it.

The mix-shift argument is real but unquantified. There is no services revenue share and no
capacity utilisation figure anywhere in the data. To defend 14.94% you need both, and the
absence is a data gap worth naming rather than a judgement worth making.

Finally, the position is inconsistent with the project's own ruling of 25 August. D-47
says a terminal excess return is dated, not capped and not exempted by archetype, and D-43
derives the horizon from the Five Forces. The paper's own decay table says a ten-to-fifteen
year moat captures only 17% to 25% of a perpetual one. This position takes a 1.68× excess
and runs it to infinity.

## Position 2 — hold capital intensity flat

### The arithmetic

Compute the opening ratio of fixed capital to revenue — $3,213m on $3,400m, or 94.5% —
and hold it. Each year's target fixed capital is that ratio times that year's revenue;
capex is whatever restores it, plus replacement of the year's depreciation. In formula
terms, capex = (target this year − actual last year) + revenue × depreciation rate.

The asset base then grows at exactly the rate revenue grows, and capital intensity at
year ten is identical to capital intensity today. The terminal is struck the same way as
in position 1 — the rate that compounds the base at *g* — but on a much larger base, so
it lands at 9.66% of revenue.

### What it produces

| | |
|---|---|
| Value per share | **$1.7676** |
| Capex, year 1 | **12.55%** of revenue |
| Capex, year 10 | 9.61% of revenue · 43.9% of EBITDA |
| Terminal capex | 9.66% of revenue · 44.1% of EBITDA |
| Invested capital ÷ revenue, opening → year 10 | 108.3% → **108.3%** |
| Fixed capital, nominal | $3,213m → $5,259m (**+63.7%**) |
| Fixed capital, in real terms | **+27.9%** |
| Terminal ROIC | **10.02%**, or **1.13× WACC** |
| Terminal reinvestment rate | 25.6% of NOPAT |
| Return on *new* capital in the terminal | 9.78% |
| Terminal share of enterprise value | 62.2% |

### DuPont

| | NOPAT margin | × | Capital turnover | = | ROIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| At the valuation date | 10.93% | | 0.9236× | | 10.09% |
| In the terminal | 10.59% | | 0.9467× | | 10.02% |

Turnover barely moves — the small rise is the working-capital component, which is held at
a fixed intensity of revenue rather than at the fixed-capital ratio. Return is essentially
flat across the whole forecast, and the terminal ROIC of 10.02% against a WACC of 8.877%
is a modest and defensible 1.13×.

### Strengths

It is the simplest rule that can be stated, and the only one that requires no view at all
about the business. Capital intensity is what it is today and stays there; the scenarios
move revenue and margin, and capital follows. That neutrality is a virtue in a framework
whose whole posture is to replace judgements with observables.

It is also what `architecture.md` §11.4.2 effectively asks for. The terminal return lands
just above the cost of capital, so the §10.6 defended-exception machinery is not engaged
and no moat has to be argued for.

And it is a genuine bound. Whatever the right answer is, it is very unlikely to require
*more* capital than holding intensity constant, so this position brackets the downside.

### Weaknesses

It requires capex of **12.55% of revenue in year one** — almost exactly the FY25 figure of
12.8% that the file explicitly rejects as inflated and non-representative. There is
something uncomfortable about a position that quietly reinstates the number two other parts
of the file spend a paragraph each disowning.

More substantively, it assumes every dollar of revenue growth needs proportional capital,
and the growth chain says that is not so. Of Denali's 6.155% nominal growth, 2.85% is
industry pricing (B29) and 3.28% is volume (B25). Price growth needs no new capacity —
only the replacement of existing assets at inflated cost. Holding intensity flat charges
the business for capacity it does not need.

It also grows intangibles at the revenue rate. The D-44 base includes $847.7m of
intangibles, largely capitalised software and acquired customer relationships. Whether
those need to compound with revenue is a real question, and this position assumes they do
without asking it.

The consequences are severe in the weak scenarios — Fragmentation falls to $0.95 and
Stagflation Persists goes negative — which is worth noticing but is not by itself an
argument. A position that makes a bad scenario look bad is doing its job.

## Position 3 — volume plus asset inflation

### The arithmetic

Split each year's revenue growth into the part that needs capacity and the part that does
not, and grow the asset base only at the first, inflated. Operationally: divide out the
chain's pricing rate to leave the volume component of that year's growth, then re-inflate
at asset inflation. Written out, the capital growth rate in year *k* is

> (1 + revenue growth in year *k*) × (1 + asset inflation) ÷ (1 + industry pricing growth) − 1

which for Muddle Through gives 5.79% in year one against revenue growth of 6.155%, and
fades as the revenue fade slows the volume term with it. Asset inflation is set equal to
terminal growth (2.50%) and is an input you can change on the Inputs sheet of the workbook.

Capex is then derived exactly as in position 2 — growth in the base plus replacement of
depreciation — and the terminal is struck the same way, landing at 9.59%.

### What it produces

| | |
|---|---|
| Value per share | **$1.8387** |
| Capex, year 1 | 12.47% of revenue |
| Capex, year 10 | 9.23% of revenue · 42.1% of EBITDA |
| Terminal capex | 9.59% of revenue · 43.8% of EBITDA |
| Invested capital ÷ revenue, opening → year 10 | 108.3% → **105.3%** |
| Fixed capital, nominal | $3,213m → $5,096m (**+58.6%**) |
| Fixed capital, in real terms | **+23.9%** |
| Terminal ROIC | **10.30%**, or **1.16× WACC** |
| Terminal reinvestment rate | 24.9% of NOPAT |
| Return on *new* capital in the terminal | 10.05% |
| Terminal share of enterprise value | 61.1% |

### DuPont

| | NOPAT margin | × | Capital turnover | = | ROIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| At the valuation date | 10.93% | | 0.9236× | | 10.09% |
| In the terminal | 10.59% | | 0.9731× | | 10.30% |

Turnover rises 5.4%, and capital intensity drifts down from 108.3% to 105.3% of revenue.
That drift is the position's actual content: a small, mechanically-derived improvement,
because the price half of growth genuinely arrives without a capital call. Terminal ROIC
of 10.30% is 1.16× WACC — an excess, but a small and explicable one.

Implied asset productivity rises 11.4% over the decade, or about 1.1% a year. That is a
number one can defend without a story.

### Strengths

It replaces a judgement with an observable, which is the move D-46 made for regulatory
settings and D-29 made for the working-capital protocol. Nobody has to hold a view about
Denali's capital intensity; the answer falls out of B25 and B29, which are already
computed, already scenario-differentiated, and already auditable through the derivation
chain.

It is scenario-differentiated by mechanism rather than by assumption. Orderly Convergence,
with 5.00% volume growth, ends at 112.3% of revenue — the scenario has to build. Stagflation
Persists, with 0.40% volume, ends at 85.4%. The six cases say something about capital
instead of all landing on the same ratio.

Every case stays below the 70% terminal-share threshold, at 50.6% to 68.4%. Position 2
pushes Disorderly Climate to 70.9% and Stagflation to 75.4%, which re-imposes the §11.4.2
sensitivity obligation that the horizon change had just lifted.

And it is replicable. Any company carrying a growth chain has a volume and price split, so
this generalises to CSL and to future companies without a per-company decision.

### Weaknesses

It is a new rule, and the check that CSL and Westpac carry a chain in the same form has not
been done. That is a condition on adopting it project-wide, not on ruling on Denali.

Asset inflation is set to terminal growth, which is a convenience rather than a derivation.
The right rate is construction and plant cost inflation, which the repository does not hold,
and the sensitivity is not trivial: 2.00% gives $1.9476 and 3.00% gives $1.7254, so half a
point either way moves the central case by about $0.11. That is *larger* than the $0.07 by
which this position beats position 2. An unsourced input carrying more weight than the
distinction it is meant to establish is an uncomfortable place for a position whose main
claim is that it has no unsourced inputs, and sourcing that rate should be a condition of
adopting it.

The construction assumes revenue growth divides cleanly into a volume rate and a price
rate that apply uniformly to the asset base. In practice a mix shift towards services does
not sit neatly in either bucket, so the split is a simplification even where the inputs are
sound.

And like position 2 it grows intangibles with the operating base, which is probably too
much capital.

## Position 4 — volume plus asset inflation, held flat

### The arithmetic

Identical to position 3, except that the capital growth rate is the chain's own volume rate
compounded with asset inflation and held constant for all ten years — (1 + 3.275%) ×
(1 + 2.50%) − 1 = 5.86% — rather than fading as revenue fades. This is how the 25 August
handover struck it, and it is preserved as a declared position rather than reconstructed
from memory.

### What it produces

| | |
|---|---|
| Value per share | **$1.6175** |
| Capex, year 1 | 12.53% of revenue |
| Capex, year 10 | **12.96%** of revenue · 59.2% of EBITDA |
| Terminal capex | 9.86% of revenue · 45.0% of EBITDA |
| Invested capital ÷ revenue, opening → year 10 | 108.3% → **116.1%** |
| Fixed capital, nominal | $3,213m → $5,692m (**+77.1%**) |
| Fixed capital, in real terms | **+38.4%** |
| Terminal ROIC | **9.35%**, or **1.05× WACC** |
| Terminal reinvestment rate | 27.4% of NOPAT |
| Return on *new* capital in the terminal | 9.12% |
| Terminal share of enterprise value | 64.3% |

### DuPont

| | NOPAT margin | × | Capital turnover | = | ROIC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| At the valuation date | 10.93% | | 0.9236× | | 10.09% |
| In the terminal | 10.59% | | 0.8833× | | 9.35% |

Turnover *falls*. Capital intensity rises from 108.3% to 116.1% of revenue, and implied
asset productivity is flat to slightly negative (−0.3%). The terminal return of 9.35% is
1.05× WACC — the closest to the cost of capital of the four, which sounds like a virtue
until you ask how it got there.

### Strengths

It is the most conservative of the four and therefore a useful floor. If a valuation
survives this treatment it is not resting on any assumption about improving capital
efficiency.

And landing within 5% of the cost of capital is exactly what a competitive-archetype
terminal ought to look like. On the framework's own logic — a mature industrial with high
buyer power and high rivalry, per the Five Forces ratings — a terminal return barely above
WACC is the theoretically expected outcome.

### Weaknesses

The construction is internally inconsistent, and this is the decisive objection. Revenue
growth fades from 6.155% to 2.50% under D-36, but the capital call is held at 5.86%
throughout. So in the back half of the horizon the asset base is compounding at more than
twice the rate of the revenue it serves, and by year ten the business is spending **12.96%
of revenue on capex** — above even the FY25 figure the file rejects — while growing at
2.50%. Nothing justifies that. It is an artefact of holding one rate fixed while another
fades, not a view about the business.

That artefact is also why the position is the lowest of the four. It is not conservative
in a principled way; it is conservative because it overspends in the years where
overspending is discounted least harshly.

The −0.3% implied asset productivity is a further tell. Over ten years, in an industry
undergoing an explicit technology transition, a business achieving *no* improvement in
output per unit of real capital is not a conservative assumption but an implausible one.

## Implications

Three things follow, and only the first is a decision.

**The choice is between positions 1 and 3.** Position 4 is an artefact of a construction
error and should be retired now that it has been reproduced and understood; it is worth
keeping only as the audit trail for the handover figure it explains. Position 2 is a
legitimate bound and should stay in the paper as one, but it charges the business for
capacity that price growth does not require, and the growth chain says how much: not quite
half of Denali's nominal growth.

Between the two survivors, position 1 asks you to believe a 73.3% improvement in output per
unit of real fixed capital over a decade, and position 3 asks you to believe 11.4%. Neither
is derivable from the repository, but only one of them is the sort of claim that could pass
without evidence. On the framework's own stated preference for observables over judgements,
position 3 is the one that does not require a view.

It is worth being clear about the shape of that conclusion. The gap between position 1 and
positions 2 and 3 is about $0.90 a share and rests on a real disagreement about the
business. The gap between positions 2 and 3 is $0.07 and is smaller than the movement
either position shows from a half-point change in an unsourced inflation rate. So the first
choice is substantive and the second is close to a tie — which argues for ruling the first
now and treating the second as a refinement that waits on better inputs.

**The 6–7% capex anchor should be promoted from an assertion to a derivation.** The
pre-demerger annual reports sit unextracted in `data/financials/historical/dnl/`. A
year-by-year capex-to-revenue series from IPL FY22 and FY23, with the fertilisers segment
identified separately if the segment note permits, would either confirm the anchor or move
it — and either outcome is worth more than the difference between two of the positions
above. It would also give `capex_pct_revenue` the `evidence_refs` that every other layer-2
judgement in the file carries.

**Whichever position is ruled, the terminal excess return still has to be dated.** D-47
settled that on 25 August and none of the four positions engages it. Position 1 carries a
1.68× excess to infinity; position 3 carries 1.16×. The second is a great deal easier to
defend, but "easier to defend" is not the same as "exempt", and D-43's Five Forces
horizon applies to both.

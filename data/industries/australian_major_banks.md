# Australian Major Banks — industry archetype

ASX-listed, APRA-regulated ADIs dominated by the Big Four (CBA, WBC, NAB, ANZ) plus regional ADIs (Bendigo, Bank of Queensland, Macquarie's banking division, Suncorp's banking-to-be-divested). Westpac Banking Corporation (ASX:WBC) is the first worked-example bank under this archetype.

## Structural position

The defining feature is the durable oligopoly. The Big Four hold roughly 73-75% of system mortgages, 60-65% of business loans, and 65-70% of household deposits. The position is reinforced by APRA's licensing regime, the funding-cost advantage of established deposit franchises, distribution scale (branch + broker + digital), and the implicit moat of being D-SIB-designated with a +1.0% CET1 surcharge that smaller competitors don't carry. The neo-bank licensing waves of 2018-2021 (Volt, Xinja, 86 400, Judo) did not displace the oligopoly: the failures (Volt, Xinja, 86 400) outnumbered the survivors, and the survivor that scaled (Judo) did so in business banking rather than mortgages.

## Five Forces summary

Buyer power runs low for retail customers (high switching frictions, transaction-banking bundling) and moderate for business and institutional customers. The binding pressure point is the marginal-mortgage cohort — those who refinance — whose price-shopping behaviour drives front-book pricing and pulls through into back-book retention pricing over time.

Supplier power is moderate. Depositors and wholesale debt investors price funding costs daily, but the Big Four are price-takers within an oligopolistic range. A bank that pushes its funding cost much below the Big Four average loses share; one that pushes much above suffers margin compression.

Threat of new entrants is low. The combination of APRA capital requirements, D-SIB surcharges for the Big Four (a moat against scaling neo-banks), funding-cost advantages of established deposit franchises, and distribution scale produces high barriers. The five-to-ten-year view is that the oligopoly is durable; existential disruption is not the central scenario.

Threat of substitutes is low to moderate. Non-bank mortgage lenders take share when bank credit-policy tightening creates an opening (the post-Royal-Commission tightening of 2018-2019 drove the most material non-bank share gain in recent history). Superannuation funds substitute for term deposits over long horizons. Fintech payments substitute for transaction-account fee revenue but not the underlying funding base. Net effect is gradual margin erosion in specific segments rather than displacement.

Rivalry is moderate and oligopolistic. Pricing discipline holds in benign periods (standard variable rate moves move within a few basis points across the Big Four, all tied to RBA cash-rate decisions). Discipline breaks under share-loss pressure: the 2022-2024 front-book mortgage cashback and discounting episode cost the industry materially, with NAB and ANZ acting as the price-aggressors and CBA / WBC defending. The break-pattern is a constant feature of the cycle — share-loss triggers a defensive price response that compresses NIM industry-wide.

## Cyclicality

The credit cycle dominates. Through-cycle credit losses for the Big Four run 15-25bps of gross loans p.a.; peak-cycle (GFC-equivalent) credit losses run 60-100bps; benign-cycle losses run 5-15bps. The current position (2025-2026) is benign — credit losses across the Big Four are running at 5-10bps with no clear single-driver crisis catalyst.

This benign position is cycle-mature rather than mid-cycle. The housing-market exuberance of 2020-2022 has cooled rather than corrected; the business-banking impairment cycle has normalised post-COVID; commercial real estate remains a tail-risk pocket but not yet a systemic one. The framework treats the current position as late-mid cycle: downside scenarios have less buffer than they would at trough.

## Capital regime

APRA's "unquestionably strong" framework: CET1 regulatory minimum 8%, plus 2.5% capital conservation buffer, plus 1.0% countercyclical buffer (currently active), plus 1.0% D-SIB surcharge for the Big Four — total floor 11.5%. Management buffers typically 50-150bps above floor. Regional ADIs (not D-SIB-designated) carry a 10.5% floor.

The capital regime binds two things: dividend payout (the residual after CET1 generation requirements are met) and asset growth (RWA growth ate CET1 generation). Banks running at the management buffer floor in stress must lower payout to rebuild capital — the canonical "dividend cuts in recession" pattern. Banks running comfortably above target distribute the excess through buybacks (commonly off-market for franking-credit reasons) or special dividends.

## Disruption vectors

Non-bank mortgage lenders are the defended-flank threat. They take 1-3pp of system flow share per credit-tightening cycle and produce a structural drag on Big Four mortgage growth even in benign periods. Reverses partially when bank credit policies loosen. Captured as a moderate threat with defender incumbency position.

Superannuation-funded member lending is the long-tail threat. Super funds entering member lending directly (mainly mortgages) is early-stage but credible on a 5-10 year view. Regulatory framework still being negotiated; outcome depends on capital-cost parity vs banks.

Open banking (Consumer Data Right) lowers retail switching frictions over time, creating asymmetric outcomes between strong-digital banks (CBA the canonical strong-digital) and weaker-digital peers. Captured as an opportunity-or-threat with neutral incumbency position.

Climate risk-weight adjustments are the regulatory-friction threat. APRA progressively incorporating climate-related credit risk into IRB models — higher risk weights on transition-vulnerable mortgages (high-flood-zone, high-bushfire-zone properties) and transition-vulnerable business book (mining, oil and gas exposures). Compresses ROE in affected portfolios; drives capital reallocation toward lower-risk exposures.

Digital-native business banking (Judo Bank, Tyro, SME fintech lenders) takes share in specific SME segments. Big Four defensive position is scale, branch network, and transaction-banking bundling.

## What feeds the company position layer

Per §15.6 of the methodology, each Big Four bank's company position is decomposed force-by-force against this archetype baseline. The positions are not symmetric across the Big Four:

CBA differentiates on the deposit franchise (lowest cost of funds), digital execution (CBA app universally cited as best-in-class), and execution discipline. ROE consistently highest in the peer group.

WBC differentiates on mortgage-book scale (#2 in system mortgage share, ~20%) — but currently runs mid-pack on cost-to-income (~48%) with execution rebuild ongoing under Anthony Miller. Business banking franchise being re-grown.

NAB differentiates on business banking strength (#1 in system business lending share) and execution under Ross McEwan / Andrew Irvine.

ANZ differentiates on the institutional / international footprint (the only Big Four with a genuine regional Asian franchise), and on the Suncorp Bank acquisition (completed 2024) which adds mortgage scale at the cost of an integration execution risk.

The company position layer translates these differentiations into quantified offsets on growth, margin, cost-to-income, and credit-loss rate, force-by-force per the §3.3 Five Forces spine.

# Moat-band fade evidence, checked against source

**Status, 24 Sep 2026: for Stephen's review — build order item 1 of `terminal_value.md`
§12.** The four citations in `terminal_value.md` §5 were gathered through a summarising
tool and flagged there as unchecked. This note fetches and quotes each source directly,
against the actual PDF rather than a description of it, and uses that to recommend
whether the band edges (§5 table) stand as proposed.

## 1. The four sources, checked

**Fama and French (2000), "Forecasting Profitability and Earnings," *Journal of
Business* 73(2), 161–175.** Abstract, confirmed via the paper's own text (SSRN working
paper / EconPapers record for the published version): *"there is economic evidence that
profitability is mean reverting in competitive environments"* and *"the rate of mean
reversion is about 38% per year"*, estimated with a partial adjustment model. The paper
adds a detail the summarising tool's citation didn't carry: reversion is **not uniform**
— it is faster when profitability sits *below* its long-run average, and faster the
further profitability sits from that average in either direction. That asymmetry is
what D-65 already leans on (Fama-French argues for a short default below the cost of
capital); it is now sourced rather than asserted. A 38%/year reversion rate implies a
half-life of about 1.4 years for the *population average* firm — materially shorter than
even the short band's five-year floor.

**Jiang and Koller (2006), "A long-term look at ROIC," *McKinsey Quarterly* 2006 No. 1.**
Confirmed via the McKinsey-hosted PDF: *"These differences in the way industries perform
haven't changed substantially over time,"* with the ROIC ranking for 1963–2004 as a whole
*"largely mirror[ing]"* the ranking for the 1995–2004 sub-period. Pharmaceuticals and
consumer packaged goods are named as durably above-average, having *"sustainable
barriers, such as patents and brands,"* while capital-intensive sectors such as basic
materials and highly competitive sectors such as retailing are named as durably below
average. This is an **industry-level** finding across four decades, not a company-level
persistence statistic — a distinction that matters for §3 below.

**Cao, Jiang and Koller (2006), "Balancing ROIC and growth to build value," *McKinsey on
Finance*, Spring 2006.** Confirmed: *"companies with an ROIC of more than 20 percent had
a 50 percent probability"* of still earning above 20% nine years later (1994 to 2003).
This is the paper's strongest single company-level (rather than industry-level or
population-average) persistence figure, and it is a **coin flip at nine years** — for the
already-elite performer, not the average one.

**Mauboussin, Callahan and Majd (2016), *The Base Rate Book*, Credit Suisse HOLT,
"Integrating the Past to Better Anticipate the Future."** Confirmed against the actual
document (Exhibit 8, p.13): the four-year persistence correlation (r) for cash-flow
return on investment is **0.78 for consumer staples** and **0.35 for energy** — the two
ends of the sector range the paper's summary quoted. The worked example (p.15) is
Microsoft, in information technology, a *middling*-persistence sector (four-year r =
0.50, sector mean CFROI 9.0% over 1983–2015 against Microsoft's 16.1%): applying the
sector's own reversion rate projects Microsoft's CFROI to 12.6% in four years, and the
text concludes *"after five years, we can assume that about one-half of Microsoft's
excess CFROI will be gone."* Excess return fell from 7.1pp to about 3.6pp — a ~49% fade
in five years, for a well-known company with real brand and platform advantages, sitting
in a sector with only average persistence.

## 2. What this says about the bands

The bands (§5 table) are short 5–10 / medium 10–20 / long 20–30 / indefinite. Three
readings, in order of how much weight they carry.

1. **The short band is not the default — it already sits above the population baseline.**
   A 38%/year reversion rate is a 1.4-year half-life for the average firm. A company with
   no named barrier does not get five years; it gets what D-58/D-43a already give it —
   convergence at or near the valuation date (N≈0). The short band is for a company that
   clears that first bar (a real, five-forces-identified advantage) but faces a named,
   already-moving threat. Worth stating explicitly in §5 so a future reader doesn't read
   "short" as "the normal case."
2. **Ten years is a genuinely calibrated line, not a round number** — it is close to where
   the *best-performing* population (>20% ROIC) is a coin flip. That argues the medium
   band's own floor is already a real assumption: surviving to 10 years on a "structural
   advantage with an identifiable erosion path" is claiming to be in the surviving half of
   the elite cohort, not an unremarkable default. The band doesn't need to move, but §5
   should say this explicitly, because it changes how the defence at the bottom of the
   medium band should read — it needs the identified mechanism, not just current
   profitability, and the Mauboussin worked example (a strong, well-known company in a
   middling sector losing half its excess in five years, not ten) is the concrete
   illustration of why current strength alone isn't enough.
3. **Long and indefinite are supported at the industry level, not the company level, and
   that gap has to be bridged by the named barrier, not assumed away.** Jiang and Koller's
   four-decade persistence is an industry average — pharma and consumer packaged goods as
   a class stay ahead — which is a different claim from any one pharma company keeping its
   own excess return for 20–30 years while rivals within the same durably-strong industry
   compete it away. The bands are still right to exist (some structural barriers — a
   regulated licence, a network — really do run decades, which is the paper's own §12a
   distinction between a rent and a barrier), but the evidence here supports the
   *industry's* durability, not a free pass for any company inside it. A long or
   indefinite band still needs D-43a's named, company-specific barrier; it should not lean
   on "the industry as a whole persists" as its own defence.

## 3. Recommendation

**Keep the band edges as proposed (5–10 / 10–20 / 20–30 / indefinite).** Nothing in the
sourced evidence argues for moving them — the McKinsey nine-year figure sits comfortably
inside the medium band's floor, and the population-average and sector-persistence figures
argue for discipline in how the bands get used, not different numbers. What changes is
the write-up: §5 should carry points 1–3 above, so the reader understands the short band
as already a step above baseline, the medium band's floor as a real (coin-flip-grade)
claim rather than a round number, and the long/indefinite bands as needing the
company-specific barrier D-43a already requires, not the industry's own track record.

# The terminal value: what it assumes, and how we choose it

**Status, 22 Sep 2026: PROPOSED. Five decisions for ratification (D-62 to D-66, §11). No
engine code has moved.** Every figure in this paper is struck on production engine output
and is regenerated by `scripts/size_terminal_options.py` into the committed set
`design/methodology/terminal_option_sets.yaml` (set `current`), which
`tests/dcf/test_terminal_option_sets.py` asserts. Figures are rounded for reading; the set
carries them in full.

Purpose: replace the component-built terminal with one that says plainly what it assumes,
lets the user change the two assumptions that matter, and reports every alternative on the
same axis so that the choice is visible rather than buried.

---

## 1. Where we started, and why we stepped back

The most common terminal value in practice is the simplest. Take the final forecast year's
free cash flow, grow it one year, and divide by the discount rate less long-term inflation:

> TV = FCF(T) × (1 + g) / (r − g), with g at the central bank's inflation target.

Call this **simple TV**. Its logic is modest, and the modesty is the point. It does not say
how the business keeps its cash flows growing with inflation — more capex, less opex, a
shift in pricing, any of a hundred combinations — because nobody can see that far. It
says only that, in aggregate, the cash keeps pace with prices. Setting g at the inflation
target rests on the reasonable expectation that the central bank does its job over the
long run.

Over the past month this project went the other way. The terminal was split into
components; capex was grown faster than revenue so that the capital base kept up with
nominal growth in perpetuity (D-49); a declared terminal return was reconciled against the
capital that roll-forward produced (D-45, D-42, M16). Each step was defensible on its
own. Together they produced a terminal that asks a user to settle, component by
component, questions about year 30 that no one can answer — which is exactly what the
terminal value exists to avoid.

What we actually want to add to simple TV is one observed phenomenon: **returns on capital
tend towards the cost of capital over time**, however that happens. Competition may force
more capex for the same profit, or the same capex may earn lower margins. We do not need
to know which. We need to know roughly how long the advantage lasts and how large it is
while it does.

This paper builds that, without going back to components.

## 2. Every terminal value is a statement about returns

Before comparing methods, one identity, because it governs all of them. For any business
growing at g, whatever the components are doing:

> g = return on new capital × share of earnings reinvested

This does not dictate the components. Reinvestment may be capex, working capital,
expensed R&D or marketing that functions as investment, or acquisitions. The return may
come from price, volume or efficiency. The identity is silent on all of that. What it
does fix is the relationship: **once a terminal value states a cash flow and a growth
rate, it has implicitly stated a return on the capital it adds.**

So simple TV, correctly understood, is not a claim about capex. It is a claim that the
cash-to-earnings ratio of the final forecast year is sustainable at growth g, and that
claim implies a return on new capital. On DNL Muddle Through that implied return is
**14.2%**, against **11.4%** earned on the whole capital base in the final forecast year
(set `current`). That may be right — the business may have ways of growing that the
forecast does not show — but it is a claim, and it should be visible.

The two-stage form below is equally silent on components. It works only in capital and
returns. The difference between the methods is not detail; it is **which two things the
user specifies and which one is implied**:

| Method | User specifies | Implied |
|---|---|---|
| Simple TV | the cash flow level, growth | return on new capital; moat length |
| Two-stage (proposed default) | moat length, return during the moat, growth | cash flow level |
| Exit multiple | a multiple of earnings | growth, return and moat, all at once |

Our preference is to have the user specify what the five-forces work can actually speak
to — how long, and how large — and to imply the rest.

## 3. The two-stage form

Let C be the capital at the end of the explicit forecast (invested capital for an
industrial, book equity for a bank), R the return earned on it, r the discount rate
(WACC or Ke), g nominal growth, and N the number of years the excess return persists
beyond the forecast.

The value of a business is its capital plus the present value of the economic profit it
earns on that capital — profit above the cost of capital. If the business earns R on a
capital base growing at g for N years and then earns exactly r:

> **TV = C + PV of N years of economic profit, C_t × (R − r)**

which collapses to a closed form:

> TV(N) = C × [ m × (1 − k^N) + k^N ], where m = (R − g) / (r − g) and k = (1 + g) / (1 + r)

Three properties make it the right default.

1. **It runs between two familiar endpoints.** At N = 0 the moat has already gone and
   TV = C: the business is worth its capital, no more. As N grows without limit, TV → C × m,
   which is the Gordon growth model at the earned return — and, for a bank, exactly the
   justified price-to-book the §15 fork already uses. The two-stage form therefore
   **nests both the current bank terminal and the current industrial terminal as its
   indefinite-moat case**, which makes migration a matter of setting a parameter, not
   rewriting an engine (§4).
2. **It needs nothing about components.** Capital, a return, a duration. The capital at the
   end of the forecast comes from the explicit years — D-44's definition and D-49's
   roll-forward stay, because C has to come from somewhere — but nothing after the
   forecast is built from parts.
3. **Once the moat ends, growth stops creating value.** With R = r, TV = C whatever g is,
   so the terminal growth rate matters only for as long as the advantage lasts. This is the
   discipline Damodaran describes: the terminal value "is not as much a function of stable
   growth as it is a function of what you assume about excess returns in stable
   growth."[^d2]

Two conventions. R is next year's earnings over capital at the end of the forecast, with
next year's earnings being the final forecast year's grown at g. And **moat length is
counted from the valuation date**, not from the end of the forecast, so that lengthening
the forecast from five years to ten (D-35) does not change the moat assumption; it only
moves years out of the formula and into the explicit forecast, where they can be reasoned
about.

## 4. What the current terminals already assume

The two-stage form can be run backwards: given any terminal value, how long must the earned
return persist to produce it? That **implied moat** is the most useful single disclosure in
this paper, and it starts by being turned on the numbers we already publish.

| Company / scenario | TV / capital | Implied perpetual return | Earned return | Implied moat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DNL Muddle Through | 1.39 | 11.4% | 11.4% | **66 years** |
| DNL AI Productivity Lag | 1.39 | 11.5% | 11.5% | **68 years** |
| DNL Orderly Convergence | 1.57 | 12.4% | 12.5% | **72 years** |
| WBC Muddle Through | 1.54 | 10.5% | 11.3% | 38 years |
| WBC AI Productivity Lag | 1.51 | 10.5% | 11.4% | 35 years |
| WBC Fragmentation | 1.41 | 10.0% | 10.4% | 42 years |
| WBC Orderly Convergence | 1.69 | 11.0% | 13.9% | 22 years |
| WBC Disorderly Climate | 1.20 | 9.0% | 9.3% | 35 years |
| WBC Stagflation Persists | 1.20 | 9.0% | 5.2% | **recovery above Ke** |

Three things stand out.

1. **DNL's terminal assumes a moat of roughly seventy years, and nobody chose it.** It is
   the arithmetic consequence of D-49 growing the capital base at g while holding the
   earned return: the component build lands within a whisker of Gordon at the earned
   return. A reviewer asked "how long does DNL's advantage last?" would not say seventy
   years. The model says it on their behalf.
2. **WBC's implied perpetual returns are the declared terminal ROEs, to the basis point**
   (10.5%, 9.0%, 10.0%, 10.5%, 11.0%, 9.0%). The bank terminal is already an
   indefinite-moat two-stage at a return below the one it earns. The design below changes
   nothing about that construction; it names it.
3. **WBC Stagflation is the one terminal that sits on the wrong side of capital** — it
   earns 5.2% against a Ke of 8.05% in the final year and then values the terminal at 1.2
   times book. That is M14, found again on the new axis without anyone looking for it,
   which is a fair test of whether the axis is useful.

DNL Fragmentation sits on convergence (TV / capital 1.00, earned 8.9% against WACC 8.9%) and
Disorderly Climate and Stagflation below it; they carry no moat to speak of, which is the
right answer. CSL cannot yet be placed on the axis at all, because its engine has no
capital base (§10).

## 5. How well can the five-forces work estimate the moat?

Not well as a point. Adequately as a band, which is all the valuation needs.

The five-forces work tells us how strong an advantage is, which force erodes it and how
fast that force is moving. It cannot turn that into a number of years on its own, and
nobody could defend "DNL's moat lasts fourteen years". Three things make this less of a
problem than it sounds.

**The value is not very sensitive to the year within a sensible range.** On DNL Muddle
Through (value per share, engine currently 1.99):

| Moat, years from valuation | 5 (converge at forecast end) | 10 | 15 | 25 | indefinite |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Value per share | 1.43 | 1.58 | 1.69 | 1.83 | 2.00 |
| TV / capital | 1.00 | 1.10 | 1.18 | 1.28 | 1.40 |

Moving from five years to fifteen is worth 0.26 a share. Moving from fifteen to forever is
worth 0.32. **The consequential question is whether the advantage ends, and only then
roughly when** — short, medium, long or indefinite. The five-forces work can answer that.

**So the input is a band, not a year.** The schema already expects this: `DecayHorizon`
(built for D-42) carries `years_low`, `years_high`, `indefinite` and a `basis`. Proposed
bands, counted from the valuation date:

| Band | Years | Typical five-forces reading |
|---|---|---|
| Short | 5–10 | advantage visible but contestable; a named threat already moving |
| Medium | 10–20 | structural advantage (scale, contracts, switching costs) with an identifiable erosion path |
| Long | 20–30 | advantage rooted in something competitors cannot readily replicate (regulated licence, network, rolling patent estate plus brand) |
| Indefinite | — | oligopoly protected by regulation or systemic scale, with no credible erosion path |

The midpoint is the default and the two ends are the sensitivity. That is honest about what
the analysis supports and it reports the range the reader should care about.

**The evidence broadly supports bands of this shape, with an important split.** Across the
population, excess profitability reverts fast. Fama and French estimate mean reversion in
profitability of about 38% a year[^ff] — a half-life of under eighteen months, on
arithmetic from their rate. But persistence is concentrated rather than uniform. Of US
companies earning ROIC above 20% in 1994, half still did so in 2003[^mck1]; persistence
differs sharply by industry, with pharmaceuticals and branded consumer goods above average
throughout four decades and basic materials and retail below[^mck2]; and in Mauboussin's
data the four-year persistence of cash returns is 0.78 for consumer staples against 0.35
for energy[^brb]. Mauboussin's own worked example has about half of an excess return gone
after five years[^brb].

The reading we take is that the typical firm's advantage fades quickly, and the firms that
keep one have something structural and identifiable. **The five-forces work exists to
decide which of those two populations a company belongs to.** That is a question it can
answer.

Calibrating the band edges against this evidence is the first research task of the build
(§12). The citations above were gathered through a summarising tool and are to be checked
against the source documents before this paper is used outside the project.

**Indefinite moats need a second lever, not a longer first one.** Stephen's instinct is
that the Australian majors' moat is indefinite, and the evidence does not contradict him.
But "indefinite at the return the bank earns today" is the most aggressive setting on the
table: WBC Muddle Through at 11.3% forever is 32.78 a share against 30.03 now. The current
construction already recognises this: it holds the moat forever and sets the return below
the one currently earned (10.5%). An indefinite moat is therefore always paired with a
return on the excess-return lever (§6), and the size of that return is where the defence
belongs.

**Returns below the cost of capital raise a symmetric question, and it needs a ruling.**
When the final forecast year earns less than the cost of capital, the two-stage form reads
N as the years the *shortfall* persists. Fama and French find that reversion is faster from
below the mean than from above it[^ff], which argues for a short default. But convergence
from below is itself a recovery assumption — exactly what M14 questioned in WBC
Stagflation — and a scenario named "Stagflation Persists" is asserting persistence. The
numbers are large:

| | Converge at forecast end | +5 years | +10 years | Shortfall forever | Engine today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DNL Stagflation Persists | 0.91 | 0.68 | 0.51 | 0.06 | 0.05 |
| WBC Stagflation Persists | 15.68 | 13.85 | 12.38 | 6.65 | 18.65 |

D-65 asks Stephen to choose. Our recommendation is that the scenario's own band governs
below the cost of capital as it does above it, with the scenario narrative required to
say which way it points. That leaves no default that is quietly optimistic.

## 6. The two levers

The default takes the band midpoint for the moat and the return the business actually
earns at the end of the forecast. A user can change either.

**Moat length** moves along the band or outside it. Outside the band, it warns.

**The excess return** can be overridden, and the direction of the override matters more
than it looks. If the user raises the return while earnings are held and capital is
solved for, value *falls* — a higher return on the same profit implies less capital was
needed to earn it, so there is less capital to earn the excess on. On DNL Muddle Through
at a ten-year moat, a 16% return would lower TV from 4,467 to about 3,728. That is correct
arithmetic and a useless lever.

The convention is therefore to **hold capital and flex earnings** (D-64): a higher return
means more profit on the same capital, and value rises with it. The difference from the
earned return is exactly the step M14's warning already reports, so the override is
policed by an existing check rather than a new one, and it enters as an input, as D-23
already allows, rather than as a second engine.

## 7. The alternatives: simple TV and exit multiples

Both stay available (D-66). Neither is vetoed. Both are reported on the same axis as the
default, so a user choosing one can see what it assumes.

**Simple TV** has a weakness that is easy to miss: it capitalises one year. Where the
forecast ends mid-glide, that year may not be representative. DNL Disorderly Climate ends
its forecast with elevated transition capex, and simple TV there is **1,925** against the
engine's **4,169** — capitalising a single abnormal year as if it were permanent. On DNL
Muddle Through the error runs the other way: the final year's capex is below the level
that keeps the capital base growing, and simple TV is 6% above the engine. Simple TV is sound
only when the final forecast year is a genuine steady state — which is one more argument
for the ten-year forecast and growth fade of D-35 and D-36 — and its implied return on new
capital is the check on whether it is.

**Exit multiples** say the most while stating the least. On DNL Muddle Through, eight times
forward EBITDA — modest by market standards — needs a perpetual return on all capital of
15.4% against the 11.4% earned; twelve times needs 21.9%. The capital base would have to be
worth two to three times its cost forever. A multiple is a useful cross-check against the
market, and a poor substitute for saying why a business will outearn its cost of capital.
A user who wants one should see what it implies before relying on it, which is the
purpose of the disclosures.

## 8. One axis: the disclosures

Every terminal, whatever its basis, is reported with the same set (D-66):

1. **Terminal value as a share of total value** — how much of the answer rests on this.
2. **TV / capital** — the implied terminal price-to-book. 1.00 means convergence; above
   that is a claim about an advantage that someone has to defend. It is also the only
   measure that reads identically for an industrial and a bank.
3. **Implied perpetual return** — the single return on all capital, held forever, that
   reproduces the TV. Equal to the cost of capital exactly when TV / capital is 1.00.
4. **Implied return on new capital** (single-stage bases only) — what the growth identity
   says the TV assumes about capital it adds.
5. **Implied forward multiples** — EV/EBIT and EV/EBITDA on year T+1 for industrials;
   P/E and P/B for banks.
6. **Implied moat** — years from the valuation date, or "perpetual or more", or "other
   side of capital" where the TV assumes a recovery (or a collapse) the forecast does not
   show.
7. **Value per share under each basis** — so the cost of the choice is in the reader's
   currency.

A further line earns its place wherever there is a market price: **what the market price
needs the terminal to be**, on the same forecast. At the reference price of 3.61, DNL's
terminal would have to carry a perpetual return on all capital of about 18.5% against 11.4%
earned — TV / capital of 2.5. Either the market sees a moat far wider than our five-forces
work does, or it sees a forecast we do not. The disclosure does not say which. It says
where to look.

## 9. What it does to the numbers

Value per share on each basis, Muddle Through (set `current`):

| Basis | DNL | WBC |
|---|---|---|
| Engine today | 1.99 | 30.03 |
| Two-stage, moat 10 years | 1.58 | 23.53 |
| Two-stage, moat 15 years | 1.69 | 25.32 |
| Two-stage, moat 25 years | 1.83 | 27.93 |
| Two-stage, indefinite at the earned return | 2.00 | 32.78 |
| Simple TV | 2.11 | 35.20 |
| Exit, 8× forward EBITDA | 2.91 | — |

Two consequences that should not be buried.

1. **A finite moat lowers both headline values materially.** A fifteen-year moat takes
   DNL from 1.99 to 1.69 and WBC from 30.03 to 25.32, about 15% each. That is the price of
   no longer assuming seventy years without saying so. Whether it is the right price is
   what the band decision is for.
2. **These are five-year figures, and the ten-year forecast will move them.** Under D-35
   the explicit forecast carries years six to ten at the forecast's own returns, so the
   moat within the forecast is no longer decided by the formula. The table sizes the
   terminal choice cleanly; it does not predict the final levels.

## 10. What this closes and what it changes

1. **M16 closes.** The earned return on the whole capital base (D-49's roll-forward) is the
   default. A declared rate (D-45) becomes an override on the excess-return lever,
   reported against the earned return. One decision amended, as M16 said it must be.
2. **D-42's materiality floor becomes moot.** The obligation to defend an excess return
   now attaches to the moat band, not to a basis-point threshold on a perpetual rate.
   Fragmentation's 4bp simply sits on convergence.
3. **The bank's terminal-return defence has a home.** The band and its basis live in the
   same `DecayHorizon` field for every construction, so the question of where a bank's
   defence goes stops being structural.
4. **M14 is reported automatically**, as §4 shows. It still needs Stephen's answer.
5. **D-53 and D-55 for CSL are absorbed.** CSL's terminal no longer depends on its
   terminal capex rule, so the 69% return on new capital that D-53 was about disappears
   with the construction that produced it. What CSL needs instead is an invested-capital
   base: D-44 on the FY25 balance sheet already transcribed (net PP&E including
   right-of-use 9,797, intangibles excluding goodwill 8,120, plus non-cash working
   capital), rolled forward through the explicit years. Until it exists, CSL reports
   every disclosure except those that need capital.
6. **The existing schema carries most of it.** `ExcessReturnDefence.decay_horizon` is the
   moat band; `declared_terminal_return` is the return lever. The D-42 work built the
   fields this design needs.

## 12. Build order, once ruled

1. **Calibrate the bands.** A sourced note on fade evidence, checked against source
   documents, fixing the band edges of §5.
2. **CSL capital base.** D-44 invested capital on the FY25 balance sheet, rolled forward.
3. **Terminal module.** One function taking capital, return, r, g, the moat band and the
   chosen basis, returning TV and the disclosure set; wired into all three engines in
   place of their terminal blocks. The formula and the inversion are in
   `scripts/size_terminal_options.py` today and move into `src/` with their tests.
4. **Schema.** The basis selector and exit multiple as scenario-overlay inputs; the moat
   band from `DecayHorizon`; the return override from `declared_terminal_return`.
5. **Re-pin.** Every level moves. Goldens are re-pinned once, with the move explained per
   scenario.
6. **Write-up.** The disclosures appear in every company write-up's terminal section, in
   the §8 order.

## 13. Limits, stated plainly

1. **The moat ends abruptly.** Returns drop from R to r in a single year. A gradual fade is
   more realistic and would make the value less sensitive again to the exact length. It is a
   refinement worth making once the structure is in place, not a reason to delay it.
2. **Capital is measured, not market.** C is book capital (invested capital, or book
   equity), so a business with large unrecorded intangibles shows a high return and a long
   implied moat. This is why the five-forces reasoning, not the ratio alone, sets the band.
3. **Bank capital is book equity, not CET1.** Consistent with the §15 fork, but a bank
   carrying surplus capital will read slightly low on the axis.
4. **Forward multiples** are on year T+1 earnings. An exit multiple entered on trailing
   earnings needs converting, and the input will say which it expects.

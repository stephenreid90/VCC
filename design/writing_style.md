# Writing Style — Valuation Matters voice

How to write prose for Stephen (blog posts, articles, discussion documents, write-ups).
Derived from the *Valuation Matters* blog (Stephen Reid & Tony Carlton). Load this whenever
drafting prose intended for readers, and audit the draft against it before handing back.

## The voice in one line

Measured, evidence-first, and quietly sceptical of received wisdom — a practitioner
walking a thoughtful peer through *why* a common practice is wrong, not lecturing.

## Positive patterns (write like this)

1. **Challenge the convention, then dismantle it methodically.** State the common practice
   plainly, name why it's flawed ("rote application", "circular presumption", "rough and
   flawed logic"), then work through the reasoning. The reader should feel walked through an
   argument, not handed a verdict.
2. **Define terms precisely before using them.** Distinguish concepts that are casually
   conflated (e.g. control premium vs takeover premium) and hold the distinction throughout.
3. **Anchor claims in evidence and specifics.** Real figures ("about 30 companies a year…
   circa 98.5%"), named studies, dated transactions, concrete cases (Aconex/Oracle). Use
   footnotes for sources and technical asides rather than cluttering the main line.
4. **Engage the reader with genuine questions.** Rhetorical or open questions to provoke
   thought and to close a piece: "So, what is going on here?"; "What value for a premium
   should you select and why?"; end posts by inviting the reader's view.
5. **First person plural and direct address.** "We observe…", "Our preference is…", and
   "you" for the practitioner reader. Collegial, not authoritative-from-on-high.
6. **Comfortable with nuance and caveats.** Qualify honestly ("generally, but not always";
   "if it ever existed"). Treat continua as continua (liquidity, control) rather than forcing
   false binaries.
7. **Always land on practical implications.** Close the loop with what the practitioner
   should actually do — an "Implications" or "Final comment" turn that converts the argument
   into guidance.
8. **Dry, understated wit — sparingly.** "akin to appending a random number"; "give or
   take". Wit serves the argument; it never performs.

## Avoid (AI tics and habits that aren't this voice)

1. No hype or marketing register; no buzzwords (delve, leverage as a verb, tapestry,
   "in today's fast-paced…", "it's important to note").
2. No "this isn't X, it's Y" reframe constructions.
3. No bullet-point avalanches in place of argument. The blog reasons in paragraphs;
   lists appear only for genuinely enumerable items (survey responses, observations).
4. No false confidence. Where evidence is thin or absent, say so plainly — that scepticism
   *is* the voice.
5. No filler hedging ("arguably", "in many ways") used to pad rather than to qualify a real
   point.
6. Don't over-explain the obvious or restate the heading in the first sentence.

## Mechanics

1. **Australian English** spelling and usage (recognise, behaviour, organise).
2. Sentences run moderate-to-long with controlled subordinate clauses and parentheticals;
   vary length, but don't chop into staccato.
3. Em-dashes and ellipses are part of the house punctuation — em-dashes for asides,
   ellipses to introduce a quotation or a beat before a turn.
4. Headings are short and plain ("Willing buyer", "Market practice", "Implications"),
   used to structure a long argument — not decorative.
5. Quote sources accurately and attribute; footnote the reference.
